The Story

We had concerns. And a wish list.

We imagined a device that knew far too much about our lives. Then we imagined never hunting for the passports again. The objections survived. So did the product concept.

It started at dinner. Nobody suggested a walk.

My wife and I were out with friends. The conversation turned to AI, as conversations apparently must now. Someone mentioned a rumored OpenAI device. None of us knew what it was. This did not slow the discussion.

Would we let it listen? Would we let it remember? Absolutely not, we said, before discussing the extremely specific things we would like it to remember.

The question followed me home:
what would it be like to live with it?

At home, my wife put on HGTV. She had selected an evening activity with a beginning, a middle, and an end. I opened the laptop.

The concept needed a camera, household memory, and access to daily life. We gave it soft lighting. Then we gave it useful things to do. The lighting was not the most effective part.

One evening later, a product that does not exist had a visual identity and an account of how it would improve your family. The future arrived first in the copy deck.

The part that gets me

The future needed softer lighting.

I used AI to imagine a device I would distrust and rely on. It understood immediately. That was helpful. That was also, on reflection, the premise.

A dinner conversation became copy, images, pages, and a working newsletter while HGTV played in the background. One of these projects involved an actual house. It was the television one.

The website is fictional. The bargain is familiar. You dislike what a device needs to know. You like what that knowledge lets it do. You keep both opinions. It keeps the appointment.

You know the problem.
You want one anyway.
